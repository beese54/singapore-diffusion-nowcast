Forecasting Singapore's flash-flood downpours with a diffusion model

It reads the last 30 minutes of rain radar and imagines eight possible futures for the next 35–95 minutes. Where the futures agree on heavy rain, there is a warning.

Why this matters

Flash floods start fast. On 27 Sep the flood site went from dry to 100 mm/hr — the top of the radar's scale — in about 25 minutes.
Alerts come when drains are already filling. PUB's flood-risk alerts go out when a drain's sensor reads 90% full — by then it is pouring. On 27 Sep this model flagged the spot about an hour before that alert, and across all 23 drain alerts in three storms it never trained on, it warned first every time (median about an hour ahead).
Minutes are what count. Half an hour to an hour of street-level notice is time to move a car or avoid a road. This prototype predicts rain, not floods, and is right about 1 time in 3 when it warns — a real signal, not yet a warning service.

How it works

  1. Radar in. NEA publishes a rain map every 5 minutes. We decode its 33 colour levels into rain rates on a 0.29 km grid and feed the model the last six maps.
  2. Noise to rain. A diffusion model starts from pure noise and removes it in 50 small steps, guided by those six maps, until a plausible future rain map remains.
  3. Eight futures. Starting from different noise gives different, equally plausible futures. The share of futures with heavy rain over a place is its forecast probability.

Watch the model draw one forecast. It is given the six radar maps below (the last 30 minutes up to 16:15) and asked to draw the rain at 16:50. The evidence

Two floods are anecdotes. The real test is 200 forecasts, each with 8 futures, spread across a 12-day period () that the model never saw in training, each compared with the naive forecast.

    When it warns of heavy rain, is it right?

    The practical question for anyone deciding whether to act: split Singapore into 2 × 2 km squares and, for every forecast in the test period, check whether heavy rain (10 mm/hr or more) really came where a warning was given.

    For researchers: overall accuracy is CRPS skill against persistence; placement is the pooled fractions skill score (FSS, 0 to 1, higher is better) within a 12 km neighbourhood. At 60–90 min CRPS alone flatters a model that spreads weak rain around, which is why both are shown. Full details in docs/RESULTS.md.

    Placement at different map scales (score, model / naive; blue = model better, red = worse)

    Did it put rain on real flood sites beforehand?

    Counts of flood reports where the forecast put rain (≥2 mm/hr in at least 4 of 8 futures) within 1.4 km beforehand. The last column checks the model is not simply raining everywhere: on ordinary days it raises an alarm at these places rarely. Only floods the model never trained on are counted, and they come from about three storms, so read these as indicative.

    A live forecast in 20 seconds

    From the newest radar map to three forecasts (30, 60 and 90 minutes ahead) × eight futures each, on an ordinary laptop graphics card. Why it matters: the radar updates every 5 minutes, so a forecast that takes 20 seconds can be refreshed with every new map, on hardware a city agency or even an individual could run.

    python src/inference/nowcast.py --checkpoint <30-min> <60-min> <90-min>

    What radar alone cannot see — and what comes next

    Heavy rain is where this model falls short: storms that form in place, or arrive from beyond the 35 × 63 km radar window, are invisible until they appear. Phase 2 adds what the model is missing:

    • Wide-range radar (240 km), collected since September, to see storms 100+ km out.
    • Himawari-9 satellite, following NVIDIA's Earth-2 Nowcasting model (StormScope), which combines satellite and radar.
    • NVIDIA CorrDiff (PhysicsNeMo) to downscale the ERA5 weather environment to ~2.5 km.
    • Learning which spots flood at what rainfall, using PUB's drain-sensor flood-risk alerts as ground truth.
    • Retraining once the archive covers the Northeast Monsoon (Dec–Mar).

    Limits, stated plainly

    • Heavy rain (10 mm/hr and more) is not forecast better than the naive forecast at any lead, and the model expects it 5–10× too weak.
    • 60 and 90 minute forecasts only help with light rain.
    • 128 days of data, one monsoon season, one 12-day test period and about three flood storms.
    • It predicts rain, not floods. PUB's flood-risk alerts only tell us when a drain passes 90% full; locations are geocoded points.
    • A research prototype — not a warning service. Follow official PUB and MSS advisories.
